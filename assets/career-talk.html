“Developer” is a family of jobs

Backend

APIs, business logic, databases. What happens after you click.

Frontend

What users see and touch, on every screen size.

QA

Breaks things before customers do.

UX / UI

Designs how it works for a human, before any code.

DevOps

Gets code to production and keeps it running.

DBA

Owns the data: speed, backups, safety.

SecOps

Thinks like an attacker, so the company doesn’t learn the hard way.

None is “better.” They’re different kinds of problems.

About 20 seconds per role. Describe the day, not the definition. Backend: the part users never see; when you place an order, they decide what happens next. Frontend: how things look, feel and respond. QA: paid to break things, thinks of the cases developers forgot. UX/UI: research, flows, prototypes. DevOps: laptop to production, pipelines, deployments. DBA: performance, backups, stopping one bad query taking the system down. SecOps: thinks like an attacker. Close: keep in mind which ones sound interesting, we'll come back to that.

Getting stuck isn’t failure. It’s where the learning happens.

"Watching tutorials feels like progress. You follow along, it works, you feel productive. But you're borrowing someone else's thinking. Any experience is experience

Your first job teaches you real teams, real codebases, real deadlines. That transfers anywhere. It’s a starting point, not a life sentence.

Investment

Long hours somewhere you’re learning a lot. This is the age to do it, while you have the energy.

Being used

Long hours repeating the same thing, for very little. Know the difference.

"Don't wait for the perfect job. Start with whatever you can get. Any experience is experience. People move from QA to development, from backend to DevOps, all the time. This is also the stage of life where you have the most energy. You can work hard, learn fast, and still have a life outside work. That gets harder later. So invest now. But one caveat: work hard in places where you're learning. Long hours somewhere that teaches you a lot is an investment. Long hours doing the same thing over and over, for very little, is just being used."

Plus availability, testing, maintainability. None of it is in the tutorial. All of it is the job.

Each card appears on a click. One: the database only holds so many connections; at 10k every request wants one, the pool runs out, and correct code fails. Know what an index does, what a slow query costs, what a transaction locks. Two: memory, disk and CPU are finite. A small leak grows until the process is killed, logs and uploads fill the disk and writes fail, one hot loop or slow query pins every core. It runs fine for hours, then dies. Profile, rotate logs, set limits. Three: bots probe within hours; brute force, injection, floods. You can't defend what you don't understand: HTTP, ports, how requests travel, TLS. Four: if your first alert is an angry customer, you're late. Logs, metrics and alerts are part of the system.

A loop inside a loop, O(n²), hides at small scale

10 records100 steps
100,000 records10 billion steps

DSA isn’t just for interviews. It decides whether your system scales or falls over.

"Underneath all of this is data structures and algorithms. A loop inside a loop, O(n squared), is invisible at 10 records: 100 steps. At 100,000 records it's 10 billion steps, and it brings your server to its knees. DSA isn't just for interviews. It's the difference between a system that scales and one that falls over."

The question in the room

Will AI take my job?

"Now, I know what some of you are thinking. Why learn any of this if AI can write the code? AI can absolutely write that API that saves a user. In seconds. But can it tell you that it will run out of database connections at 10k requests? That it charges someone twice when they double-click? Only if someone knows to ask, and knows whether the answer is right."

AI types. You decide.

01

You direct

You tell it what to build, and how.

02

You judge

Is the answer actually right? Will it survive 10k requests?

03

You own it

When it breaks in production, it’s on you, not the AI.

Use AI heavily. Never ship code you can’t explain.

"AI is the one typing. You are the one deciding. You tell it what to build. You judge whether what it gave you is correct. And when it goes to production and something breaks, you're the one responsible. You can't give good directions, and you can't catch a bad answer, without the fundamentals. So AI doesn't make the fundamentals less important. It makes them worth more. Use it to learn, to explore, to review your code. But never ship code you can't explain."

"The most valuable skill I've seen in engineers isn't knowing the most frameworks. It's being an investigator.
